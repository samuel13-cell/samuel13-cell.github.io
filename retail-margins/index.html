Where the margin went

Fourteen years of India’s Wholesale Price Index for one value chain — raw hides, tanned leather, finished footwear — and what the gap between what a maker pays and what a maker charges says about margin.

The finding

Input prices
+5.2% Raw hides and skins, April 2012 to April 2026
Finished goods
+29.9% Footwear and leather shoes, same period
Spread
123 Finished prices relative to inputs, +23 points against a base of 100

Input prices end the period barely five percent above where they started — but they did not get there quietly. Raw hides and skins swung through a range of roughly twenty index points along the way, with no sustained direction. Finished-goods prices, by contrast, rose about thirty percent and did so steadily. The gap between those two lines is the room a manufacturer or retailer has to work in.

That runs against the usual assumption that producers are squeezed by rising raw material costs. In this chain, over this period, input volatility was the problem to manage — not input inflation.

The spread itself

The rise is not smooth. The spread sat flat near 100 until 2015, stepped up sharply through 2016–17, held, then stepped up again through 2021–23. Those are the two periods worth asking about — and the most recent readings have started to fall back, which is the part that matters for anyone buying leather today.

Method

The Office of the Economic Adviser publishes the Wholesale Price Index monthly, with each commodity carrying a fixed weight in the basket. Five series make up this chain: hides and skins on the input side, tanned leather in the middle, and footwear and leather shoes as finished goods. Input and output stages are weight-averaged into one series each, then both are rebased to 100 at April 2012 so a single axis carries both.

The spread is simply one rebased index divided by the other. It is a ratio of prices, not a measured margin: it says how far selling prices have moved relative to buying prices, which is the part of margin that prices can explain.

What this does not show

These are national indices. They describe an industry, not a business. They exclude labour, rent, energy and finance, which are real costs and move differently. A single firm negotiating with a single tannery may see none of this. And the 2011-12 base period is now old enough that the basket's weights reflect an economy that has since changed — the Office of the Economic Adviser has published a 2022-23 base for exactly that reason, which this page does not yet use because it only reaches back to 2023.

Direction is the signal here. Level is approximate.
