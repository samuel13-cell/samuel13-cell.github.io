The Impact Player effect

In 2023 the IPL let each side swap in a substitute mid-match. Scoring jumped immediately. The interesting question is not whether it rose. It is which part of the innings moved, because that points at why.

If batters had simply got stronger, through better bats, shorter boundaries and the general drift of T20, then the death overs should have risen most. That is where sides already swing hardest, so extra power shows up there first. Instead the powerplay rose most, by @@pp_delta@@ runs an over against @@death_delta@@ at the death.

The powerplay went from the slowest phase of an IPL innings to nearly the fastest. That is not a change in how hard players hit. It is a change in how much risk they are willing to take early, which is exactly what having an extra batter in reserve buys you.

Method

Cricsheet publishes every delivery of every IPL match as open data. This uses all @@balls@@ of them across @@matches@@ matches, @@first@@ to @@last@@, aggregated in DuckDB. Phases follow the competition's own structure: the six-over powerplay, the middle overs, and the last five. Runs per over counts every run conceded, byes and wides included, since those are runs the fielding side gave away.

“Before” is every season up to 2022 and “after” is @@rule_season@@ onward. The rule applied from the first match of @@rule_season@@, so the split needs no judgement call.

What this does not show

This is an association in observational data, not a controlled experiment. The rule was not assigned at random and 2023 changed more than one thing at once: squads turn over, new players arrive, grounds and conditions vary by season. A general rise in T20 batting standards is real and is part of this.

What the phase breakdown does is narrow the field. A pure power story predicts the death overs move most; they moved least. That is evidence for a change in risk-taking rather than in hitting ability. It remains evidence, not proof, and one competition over four seasons is a small sample to hang a causal claim on.
